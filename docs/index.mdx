The first minute of a rummy hand decides more than most players admit. Thirteen cards land in front of you in no order at all, and while you are still reading them one by one, the table has already moved. The fix is not speed. It is a habit: sort the hand into three piles before you think about a single move.

![Sorting the first hand into three piles](/assets/sorted-piles.jpg)

## Pile one: the pure run, or its bones

Every 13-card hand needs a pure sequence — a run of three or more cards in one suit, with no joker standing in. So the first pile is anything that already looks like a run, or sits one card away from one: the 4-5-6 of spades, the 9-10 of hearts waiting for a jack or an 8. These cards are spoken for. You do not throw from this pile, and you do not break it to chase a prettier idea elsewhere.

## Pile two: pairs and honest maybes

The middle pile holds pairs, near-pairs and cards one step from a set or a second run. This is the pile you are still deciding about. It is allowed to change: a pair can die quietly when its third card shows up in the discard pile twice, and a maybe can graduate to pile one the moment its missing neighbour arrives. Keeping the maybes together means you re-decide them as a group instead of re-reading the whole hand every turn.

## Pile three: the honest leftovers

Whatever is left is your discard stock — cards with no run, no pair and no plan. Naming them honestly is the whole point. A leftover is not a failure; it is tomorrow's throw, chosen in advance instead of in a panic. When your turn comes, you already know which end of the hand you are throwing from, and your fingers stop advertising your uncertainty to the table.

Three piles beat one long row for a simple reason: a row must be re-read, a pile only re-checked. The sorted hand also protects you from the two classic beginner accidents — discarding a card that belonged to your run, and holding a dead card for six turns because it was hiding between two useful ones.
